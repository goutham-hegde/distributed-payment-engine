The problem

In one database this is trivial. Across three services it is not.

Alice has ₹1,000. Bob has ₹500. Alice sends Bob ₹300. Inside a single database that is one transaction and there is nothing to discuss. Split the work across three services that can each fail on their own, and every one of these becomes possible:

  • The account service crashes after debiting Alice, before crediting Bob₹300 vanishes
  • The network drops the message that credits Bob₹300 vanishes
  • The network delays it, the client retries, and it lands twiceBob gets ₹600
  • Two transfers read Alice’s balance at the same momentAlice goes negative
  • The card processor times out mid-chargenobody knows the outcome

This project implements the standard industrial answer to each — saga orchestration, a transactional outbox and inbox, idempotent consumers, a double-entry ledger — and then attacks it to find out which answers were incomplete. Five of them were.

      How correctness is proven

      Not by HTTP 200s

      A service can answer every request successfully and still have lost your money. So the system is judged by what is in its ledger. Five invariants are asserted after every fault injection and every load run:

       InvariantIn plain terms
      I1SUM(ledger_entries) = 0No money was created or destroyed anywhere in the system.
      I2balance = SUM(its entries)Every stored balance still agrees with the entries behind it.
      I3balances + holds constantThe total is the same at the end of a run as at the start.
      I4no saga left non-terminalNothing is stuck half-done once the system goes quiet.
      I5no customer balance < 0Nobody was overdrawn, however concurrent the traffic was.

      The five invariants turned out not to be enough

      The chaos suite’s first run found five saga defects. Only one of them tripped any of I1–I5. The rest passed all five while money sat stranded mid-flight, or while the card network held a charge for a payment that had been refunded. Money can be perfectly conserved inside your own books and still be wrong about the outside world.

      Four more checks now run alongside them, each joining two services’ databases:

       CheckWhat it catches
      S1No active hold once the system is at restMoney parked mid-flight with nothing left alive to move it — which I3 counts as conserved.
      S2Every approved charge belongs to a completed transferA customer refunded in our ledger while the card network kept the charge.
      S3Every completed transfer has an approved chargeMoney moved without the payment provider ever agreeing to it.
      S4No pending transfer under a finished sagaA customer told “processing” about something that finished long ago.
      What breaking it found

      Five defects, in code whose tests were all green

      Each scenario states a hypothesis, injects one fault into the running stack — a kill signal, a frozen container, a network cut aimed at an open transaction, a processor that declines or answers twice — waits for the system to go quiet, then asserts both the invariants and the outcome the design promised.

      1. A refund that paid the customer out of our own books

        A timeout after the card was charged recovered the same way as a timeout before it: by unwinding. The customer got their money back and the card network kept the charge. Every one of I1–I5 stayed green throughout. The charge is now the saga’s pivot. Before it, recovery runs backward; after it, only forward — the system retries the step it already committed to rather than unwinding something the outside world has already done.

      2. A participant that answered with silence

        Asked to act on a hold that had already been settled, the account service logged the fact and replied with nothing, so the saga could only ever learn from its own deadline. It now replies with what actually happened to the hold. A reply is a statement of fact, not an acknowledgement — and silence was never the third option.

      3. A compensation that raced the step it was compensating

        A timeout is decided from a clock, not from the message order, so it can fire before the step it is cancelling has even arrived. The cancellation sent nothing, the late step landed anyway, and the money stayed stranded mid-flight forever. Compensations are addressed by transfer and leave a tombstone behind, so they commute with the step they compensate — whichever one arrives first.

      4. A replayed dead letter that charged a cancelled payment

        A message rescued from the dead-letter queue and replayed went on to charge a transfer that had already been refunded. Fixed the same way, at the gateway: a cancelled transfer leaves a tombstone that a later charge collides with instead of overwriting.

      5. A restarted service that timed out payments it could already hear

        After a crash the orchestrator served traffic and ran its timeout sweeper for roughly 23 seconds before its message consumer rejoined the group — and failed payments whose replies were sitting unread, waiting for it. The sweeper now waits until the reply listener actually holds its partitions. A service must not declare things dead while it is deaf.

      The overloaded run is the interesting one. The system stays correct and stays fast for what it accepts, and refuses the excess at the front door instead of accepting it and timing it out thirty seconds later. Getting there meant finding a metastable collapse first: past about 15 payments a second, customers’ status polls took every database connection the payment pipeline needed, payments timed out, their compensations added more work, and the system stayed down after the load stopped — with every paisa still conserved. Capacity went from ~15/s to ~38/s.

      Architecture

      Three services, three databases, one conversation

      Each service owns its database and nothing else. No service reads another’s tables; they talk only through message topics. Every arrow below is a row written to an outbox in the same transaction as the change it reports, published by a relay, and de-duplicated on arrival.

      payment-orchestratorThe public API, the saga state machine, idempotency, tokens.
      account-serviceThe money. Double-entry ledger, holds, and the clearing accounts.
      payment-gatewayA simulated card processor, with failure injection you can turn on at runtime.
      • POST /api/v1/transfersOne transaction: the idempotency claim, the admission check, the transfer, the saga and the first command — then 202 Accepted. No money has moved.
      • ReserveFundsOne transaction: lock both accounts in id order, debit the sender, credit a clearing shard, open a hold, write the reply.
      • ChargeGateway → approvedThe pivot. Before this point recovery runs backward; after it, only forward.
      • CommitFundsDebit clearing, credit the recipient. The payment is complete.
      • ReleaseFundsThe compensation, on a decline or a timeout before the pivot: debit clearing, credit the sender back.

      Money in flight lives somewhere

      Between the debit and the credit, the money sits in a clearing account — never nowhere. Completing and cancelling a payment both write the identical ledger entry against that account, under a uniqueness constraint. So doing both to the same payment is impossible in the schema, not merely something the orchestrator promises not to do.

      A note on “exactly once”

      This system does not provide exactly-once delivery, because that is impossible over an unreliable network. A crash between “the broker acknowledged” and “the row is marked published” republishes. What it provides is at-least-once delivery with idempotent consumers, which gives effectively-once processing. The distinction is the whole design.

      One open issue, recorded honestly

      After a broker restart, a message consumer occasionally stops fetching while its group still reports it healthy. It reproduced in 4 of 10 restarts on one broker implementation and 0 of 13 on another, with identical client code. The root cause is not established, and it would be wrong to describe it as known. Since the fixes above it strands no money; an alert catches it and a restart clears it.
